# Tantric traditions: sites, texts and interpretations

**Odisha’s Yogini temples tell a story through circles, sculpture and the relationship between goddesses and Shiva.** Start with two distinct places: [Hirapur](../places/hirapur-yogini.md) in Khordha and [Ranipur-Jharial](../places/ranipur-jharial-yogini.md) in Balangir. Their open-air architecture gives this heritage collection a strong visual identity without needing claims of national uniqueness.

## Three contexts to distinguish

**Yogini** names a category of divine figures and associated traditions; it does not identify one uniform religion or modern yoga practice. **Shakta** describes a goddess-centred emphasis, while **Shaiva** refers to Shiva-related traditions. At these sites, Yogini and Bhairava/Nataraja imagery should be read together, without requiring the two emphases to be mutually exclusive.

**Buddhist Vajrayana** has its own Yogini-tantra traditions. Shaman Hatley’s openly available scholarly introduction discusses both Shaiva Bhairava traditions and Buddhist Yogini texts. Shared terminology creates a subject for comparison; it does not establish that either Odisha temple was a Buddhist monastery or that all these traditions followed one lineage. [Hatley, 2020, Introduction](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62357/reference-article-abstract/554513529).

## A textual connection worth investigating

Sabbi Lall’s SOAS report of Hatley’s 2020 lecture describes possible parallels between Hirapur’s images and goddesses in the Devīpurāṇa. The reported argument stops short of asserting that the text inspired the temple. Keep this as an **attributed iconographic hypothesis**, with the text’s date, edition and relevant passages still to examine. The lecture recording and original Sanskrit passages have not been reviewed here. [SOAS report, 14 January 2021](https://blogs.soas.ac.uk/yoga-studies/2021/01/14/in-search-of-yoginis/). The report and Hatley’s Oxford entry share a scholar; they are not independent confirmation.

## What stays open

- **Hirapur’s date and patronage:** Odisha Tourism’s saved account gives the ninth century and Hiradevi; IGNCA argues early tenth century and names the Bhaumakaras generally. Preserve both attributions pending inscription-level or specialist review.
- **Ranipur-Jharial’s image count:** the inventory states 65 niches, but its categories total 66 (48 + 11 + 7). No corrected count is invented, and no current surviving-image total is published.
- **Ritual and access:** IGNCA labels Hirapur living and Ranipur-Jharial non-living, without a survey date. These labels cannot establish present practice, availability or an unbroken ritual tradition. Its brief Kapalika attribution is not enough to reconstruct historical rites.
- **Text and transmission:** direct passage-level links with Buddhist or Jagannath traditions remain unestablished. No secrecy-based promotional claims, supernatural claims as fact, or undocumented origin chain is adopted.
