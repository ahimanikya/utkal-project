# Taste Odisha: a collection of kitchens

A food journey can begin with the person who shows you how something is made.

In a 2019 interview by **Mansita Mishra**, chef **Thomas Zacharias** recalls learning about Odia cooking at **Alka Jena’s** home, after an introduction through fellow chef Rachit Kirtiman. He describes a later collaboration with Jena on an Odisha-inspired restaurant menu. A home cook remains visible in the story of how that food travelled.

That is a good place for *Taste Odisha* to begin. Follow a dish back towards the people who prepare it. Let a familiar ingredient surprise you. And leave space for the next cook to do something differently.

## Let the whole meal speak

[Pakhala](../food/pakhala.md) offers one starting point. Odisha Tourism describes a version of rice fermented in water, accompanied by fried vegetables and fish, and associates it with summer. The rice is only part of what you might want to understand: look at what has been placed beside it, and ask how the cook likes to bring the meal together.

In Mishra’s November 2019 menu account, pakhala appears with curd and a ginger-and-mustard tempering. The same menu’s **Habisa Dalma** combines moong with raw banana, arbi and elephant apple. These are dated restaurant descriptions, not instructions for every household or proof of a ritual kitchen’s practice.

When choosing a meal, ask what is being prepared that day. If a cook has time to talk, a small question can open more than a long list: which accompaniment would they miss most if it were absent? What changes when they cook for themselves rather than for guests? Their answer belongs to that person, and might be the part you remember longest.

Our [dalma research](../food/dalma.md) and [preparation collection](../food/preparation-and-variation.md) keep different accounts side by side. You do not have to settle on a single definitive plate before enjoying the conversation.

## A leaf can change what you notice

**Enduri pitha** invites you to look at the wrapping as well as the filling. In **Dr Lipi Das’s** 2015 ICAR compilation, rice-and-black-gram batter and a sweet filling are enclosed in turmeric leaves and steamed. The book also calls it *Haldi Patra Pitha*. Odisha Tourism connects enduri with **Prathamastami**.

If you have an opportunity to try a freshly prepared serving, pause over its shape, surface and aroma. Ask the maker about the leaf and filling they used. Those details give the food a particular identity; “rice cake” alone leaves much unexplained.

The book credits **Ananya Mahila Bikas Samiti** at Sankilo, Cuttack, for information on several Odisha foods, alongside secondary sources. That acknowledgement does not establish that each recipe came from Sankilo.

## Follow the method through the pitha collection

The same book gives several ways to turn rice into something worth stopping for. These are selected preparations in the publication, rather than universal recipes or an exhaustive pitha list.

| Preparation | What to look for in this source’s version |
| --- | --- |
| [Chakuli](../food/chakuli.md) | Batter spread and cooked on a pan; the book includes an onion-and-herb version. |
| [Enduri](../food/enduri-pitha.md) | Filled batter folded inside turmeric leaves, then steamed. |
| [Manda](../food/manda-pitha.md) | Filled rice dough shaped and steamed. |
| [Kakara](../food/kakara-pitha.md) | A rice-and-wheat-flour casing with sweet coconut filling, fried. |

Compare two preparations if they are available. What changes when a batter is spread rather than enclosed? What does a filling add? You can build a tasting around those questions without trying to finish an entire culinary checklist.

Names alone do not settle every method. Odisha Tourism calls its *sijha manda* boiled; the ICAR version above is steamed. Our research retains that distinction for local clarification. Ask the cook what they call their preparation and how they make it. A useful food notebook has room for more than one answer.

## Leave room for a changing sweet

[Chhena poda](../food/chhena-poda.md) brings the journey to a baked sweet. Odisha Tourism describes sweetened fresh-curd cheese baked under sal leaves in an earthen oven. That is one documented preparation to read alongside the slice you encounter.

In a contribution published on 29 July 2021, **Chef Michael** offers a chocolate adaptation using chhena or paneer. It is explicitly his contemporary recipe. You can be curious about an adaptation while still wanting to understand the preparation that inspired it.

Ask what the maker has kept, what they have changed, and why. Compare the surface with the centre; give your own impressions words before borrowing someone else’s verdict. This is an invitation to taste attentively, not a ranking of cooks or a verdict on which version alone belongs to Odisha.

## Bring an appetite—and a little time

For a first food outing, choose a manageable focus: one meal, two pitha preparations, or a sweet and the story of its maker. Check that the place currently serves what you want before travelling specifically for it. Historical articles and recipe pages do not establish today’s menu, hours or availability.

Ask about the ingredients in the actual serving if you have dietary needs; a dish name is not enough. If you would like a photograph, a recording or a recipe to share, ask permission and keep the cook’s credit attached. A private home mentioned in a published interview is not an open visitor venue.

Keep three things from the experience: the name of the preparation, who made or explained it, and one detail you would tell a friend. The next kitchen may give you another version. That is a reason to keep exploring.
