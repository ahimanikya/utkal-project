# Rice kept for taste, seed saved for tomorrow

Before asking what goes beside the rice, ask which rice is on the plate. That small question opens a different way into Odisha: through the food someone wants to keep cooking, the seed they choose to keep, and the work needed for another sowing.

A bowl can be a beginning rather than the end of a story. Follow it towards Koraput’s seed keepers and a women-led seed enterprise in Kalahandi, with a pause for the particular pleasures people associate with particular rice names.

## The rice someone wants to cook

In a study published in February 2026, Preeti Yadav and colleagues recorded culinary preferences among selected farming communities in western Odisha and Koraput. Participants associated Kalahans with kanika and pakhala, and Sapuri with popped rice for weddings. These are reported local uses, not rules for every kitchen. The names do not establish that two seed lots are genetically identical.[1]

The choice offers a question to carry into a conversation: what would change in this meal if another rice were used? Leave room for the cook’s answer. A flavour, a texture or a family occasion may matter in a way that a list of varieties cannot explain.

## Tulasa Ghiuria and the next sowing

An account hosted by the M. S. Swaminathan Research Foundation places Tulasa Ghiuria in Nuaguda, Kundura, Koraput. It describes her conserving more than 70 indigenous rice varieties and cultivating Kalajeera, Dangarbasumati and Machhakanta. The page credits first publication to 26 July 2022; it is a dated account of her work, not a current seed catalogue.[2]

Read the names slowly. They give the story a person and a place, and make seed conservation something more tangible than a broad appeal to save heritage. The next question is practical: how does a grower tell them apart, and what makes each worth keeping? Asking gives the person doing the work room to explain what a packet label cannot.

## Women producing seed in Kalahandi

There is another kind of rice story in Kalahandi. IRRI’s May 2020 report describes Adarsh Dharmagarh Women Farmers Services Producer Company. It reports that around 180 women produced about 350 tons of paddy seed in 2019. Pooja foundation seed was among the material supplied to growers.[3]

That is seed production, not a tally of rice sold for meals. The account concerns a seed enterprise; it does not establish present operation or members’ profit. Alongside conservation, it opens a separate question: who makes seed available for the next crop, and what work makes that possible?

## A conversation to bring home

You do not need to arrive with a catalogue of correct answers. At a meal, begin with the name used by the person serving it. If they are willing to talk, ask how they choose the rice for that dish and who taught them the preparation. A remembered difference is worth listening to without turning one household’s answer into a claim about an entire region.

If a grower or seed keeper chooses to share their knowledge, ask what they would like a visitor to understand. Agree on credit and permission before recording, photographing or publishing their words. A source profile is an introduction to someone’s work, not permission to visit their home or request seed.

For a notebook, keep four things together: the rice name as given, the broad place, the dish or use, and who shared the account with permission. Record questions alongside answers. A careful conversation can leave you with more than a souvenir: a reason to notice the next bowl differently.
