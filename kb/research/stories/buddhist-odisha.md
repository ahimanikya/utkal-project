# A name in clay across Odisha’s Buddhist circuit

A monastery can return to us through a few letters.

At Udayagiri-2, excavated clay seals and sealings helped researchers identify **Simhaprastha Mahavihara**. Among the published readings is a fragment carrying part of that name. The archaeological report preserves its accession number, the reading and the uncertainty around damaged lettering. A place that might first appear as walls and foundations becomes a named institution, remembered through something small enough to overlook.

That is a rewarding way to approach Odisha’s Buddhist circuit: let one detail hold your attention before widening the view. [Ratnagiri](../places/ratnagiri.md), [Udayagiri](../places/udayagiri-buddhist.md) and [Lalitgiri](../places/lalitgiri.md) offer related histories, but each deserves its own questions. You do not have to recognise every figure or understand every period to begin looking closely.

## Let the name lead you

The ASI report on the 1997–2000 excavations, by **Bimal Bandyopadhyay**, credits the inscription readings to **Snigdha Tripathy**. It distinguishes Simhaprastha at Udayagiri-2 from **Madhavapura Mahavihara**, identified through seals in an earlier excavated area at Udayagiri.

Two names within the same archaeological site are already a reason to slow down. Ask how a particular structure, object or inscription acquired its identification. A guide or museum label may be able to connect what you see with the evidence behind the name. A question left open is also worth remembering; it can lead you back to a book after the visit.

Keep the destination clear when arranging transport. This Buddhist Udayagiri in the Jajpur circuit is distinct from the Jain Udayagiri–Khandagiri caves near Bhubaneswar. A familiar place name is not always a sufficient address.

## Three places and different ways of looking

For **Ratnagiri**, begin with the work of discovery. The Udayagiri report recalls **D. Mitra’s excavations at Ratnagiri between 1958 and 1961**. That research history gives another dimension to a visit: someone had to expose, record and interpret the remains before they could become the heritage landscape a visitor encounters.

Choose a detail that interests you, then look for its context. Where was the object found? Does the label give a date, an interpretation or both? If photography is permitted, a picture of the label alongside the object can preserve a connection that a dramatic close-up alone would lose.

At **Lalitgiri**, the containers around a relic offer a different point of attention. A Ministry of Culture briefing issued in December 2018 describes three khondalite stone caskets recovered from a stupa during the 1985 excavations. Two held further caskets of steatite, silver and gold, with charred bone relics inside. The same briefing describes the new site museum as a place for displaying and protecting excavated antiquities.

The sequence of materials invites a patient look: stone, another stone container, silver, gold. What did enclosing and protecting something mean to the people who placed it there? Let interpretation accompany that question. The account establishes a reported find; it does not establish whose remains the relics are. Religious significance can be discussed respectfully without attaching a certainty the evidence has not supplied.

Museum arrangements and individual displays can change. Check access before setting out, particularly if the caskets or a specific object are the reason for your visit.

## Read a traveller beside the archaeology

There is another kind of journey in **Xuanzang’s account**, available in English through **Li Rongxi’s translation**. In its description of Uḍa, the text places an interest in learning alongside more than a hundred monasteries and over ten thousand monks. It then introduces Pushpagiri monastery and reports spiritual signs associated with its stupa.

Read those passages with their different purposes in mind. The numbers refer to the wider historical country, not to a student register for Pushpagiri. The spiritual signs belong to the traveller’s religious account. Neither passage identifies Simhaprastha merely because both belong in a story about the region.

Our [Buddhist learning collection](../history/buddhist-learning.md) follows these connections and the debated identification of Pushpagiri. A modern circuit makes it easier to discover several places; it does not turn them into one ancient university. The more carefully we keep their identities, the more interesting the connections become.

## Make room for one good explanation

Before choosing a route, decide what you most want to understand: a monastery’s name, a sculpture, the care of relics or the history of excavation. Choose one main site and allow time for its museum or interpretation if available. Add another only after checking transport, current opening arrangements, walking conditions and your return time. This story is a reading companion, not a tested one-day itinerary.

Ask a qualified guide to explain one object or structure in detail. Establish the appointment and fee beforehand; confirm any language or mobility needs. Keep a respectful distance from remains, follow site instructions and ask about photography. If an explanation includes local belief, record it as that person’s account, with permission to credit them.

The photograph you keep might show a broad view. The memory beside it could be much smaller: a material, a name, or the moment a few damaged letters made a place feel particular.
