# Dhauli and the instructions to power

**Give the white Peace Pagoda its moment. Then make time for an older voice in the landscape, concerned with what happens to people in the hands of officials.**

A visit to Dhauli can begin with a view and continue with a sentence.

The inscriptions belong to the third century BCE. The white Shanti Stupa belongs to 1972. Keeping those dates apart makes the place more interesting: different generations have given this landscape different ways of speaking about power and peace.

The older words are in Prakrit, written in Brahmi script. You do not have to be able to read the letters to begin reading the questions they raise.

## The people behind the proclamation

“All men are my children,” declares the king in Ven. S. Dhammika’s English rendering of the first Kalinga Rock Edict. The passage addresses judicial officers at Tosali. It urges impartial treatment and warns against unjust imprisonment and harshness.

The sentence becomes more demanding when you consider its audience. It is addressed to people who exercise authority over others. For a visitor today, that creates a question with unusual staying power: what would care look like in the way an official does an ordinary day’s work?

This is an encounter with a ruler’s stated intention. The instruction itself does not prove how every official behaved or how every subject experienced his rule. That distinction keeps the people affected by power in the story, alongside the person who ordered the inscription.

## Which words belong to this rock

Dhauli contains Major Rock Edicts I–X and XIV and two Separate Rock Edicts. Major Rock Edict XIII, widely associated with the account of suffering after the conquest of Kalinga, is absent here. Dhammika’s rendering identifies its translated version as the Kalsi text.

This is a useful detail to carry into a guided visit. Ask which inscription a quotation comes from. A story about Ashoka can range across his inscriptions; a caption identifying the words on one particular rock needs to be more exact.

The familiar river story has a different place in the telling. Odisha Tourism has retold the legend of the Daya turning red during the war. It is an image of remembered suffering, rather than a measured description of the river or a surveyed boundary of a battlefield.

## Look slowly at the elephant

Above the inscriptions, the front of an elephant emerges from the rock. Let your eye follow the meeting of the carved figure and the stone around it. You can appreciate the form without turning one interpretation of its symbolism into the only possible meaning.

Keep to the permitted viewing area and leave the carving untouched. A photograph can preserve what you noticed; a note can preserve the question that remains.

Before travelling, check the approaches to the inscription site and the pagoda separately. Ask about walking surfaces, steps, rest points and your return journey. Give each stop enough attention that the older inscription does not become a hurried footnote to the view.

Leave with one question for the ride home: when a place remembers peace, how much room does it make for the people who needed protection?
